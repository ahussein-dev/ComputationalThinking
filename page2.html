Famous Computer Scientist

Joan Clarke was a mathematician mostly known for her role in cracking the Enigma code. The Enigma was a cipher machine the German military used to encrypt its communications, including messages to and from U-boats. [5]

Place of birth:
West Norwood, London, UK
Date of birth and death:
24 June 1917 – 4 September 1996
Education:
Newnham College, Cambridge
Employment:
  • Government Code and Cypher School, Bletchley Park
  • Government Communications Headquarters (GCHQ)[6]
[7]

Biography

Joan Clarke studied Mathematics and was awarded a double first in 1940. Her supervisor persuaded her to come to Bletchley Park, after the completion of her studies. When Clarke initially joined Bletchley, she was assigned clerical work and paid significantly less than her male counterparts.

However, within a few days, her abilities shone through and she started work in Hut 8. She worked alongside the famous Alan Turing. Clarke was the only woman on this team where the Enigma code was broken.[8] The ciphers that Clarke and her colleagues broke were the most complex that Bletchley Park had worked on. Hut 8's work led to immediate action against U-boats: by November 1941, shipping losses had fallen from 282,000 tons a month to 62,000 tons. Clarke became one of the best practitioners of Banburismus, a statistical technique devised by Turing that reduced the number of bombe runs needed to break the daily settings, and in 1944 she became deputy head of Hut 8.[9] Her work helped shorten the war and save many lives on both sides of the conflict.

Clarke was appointed MBE in 1946 for her work during WW2, but Clarke's work on the Enigma project was rarely acknowledged, along with other female codebreakers that worked at Bletchley. In addition to this her work is significantly overshadowed by her relationship to Alan Turing. Articles and biographies of Clarke tend to focus on her engagement rather than her work. Not only has she inspired women within the computer science and tech field, but she has also inspired women to join male dominated fields such as engineering. She inspires women to believe they are capable of achieving higher than expected just like she did. Her work still impacts the world we live in today as the Chief Security Officer, Sunila Shivpuri said “While Joan Clarke used her codebreaking skills to help defend her country during wartime, codebreakers in the present day sometimes pursue more criminal objectives.”[10]
